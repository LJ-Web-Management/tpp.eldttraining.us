Spanish-Language ELDT Training: Options for Bilingual Drivers and Employers

Spanish-speaking workers are a large and growing share of the transportation workforce. Bilingual ELDT theory helps those drivers master complex safety material, as long as employers also plan for federal English language proficiency requirements.

Updated October 25, 2026 3 min read By the HAZWOPER OSHA Training team, an FMCSA TPR-listed ELDT theory provider

Key Takeaways

  • The ELDT rule doesn't restrict the language theory is taught in, and bilingual courses help drivers understand complex safety and regulatory material.
  • Separately, commercial drivers must meet FMCSA English language proficiency requirements, which are actively enforced at roadside inspections.
  • The strongest approach pairs bilingual theory with English proficiency support, so drivers get both comprehension and compliance.

Can ELDT theory be taken in Spanish?

The FMCSA ELDT rule sets curriculum and assessment requirements but doesn't restrict the language of instruction. Many registered providers offer Spanish versions of theory courses, and some, like HAZWOPER OSHA Training, include an English/Spanish toggle on every course so drivers can switch languages at any time.

A toggle is particularly useful for bilingual learners. A driver can read a hours-of-service module in Spanish to understand the concept, then switch to English to learn the exact terms they'll see on logs, signs, and inspection reports.

English language proficiency still applies

Federal regulations at 49 CFR 391.11(b)(2) require commercial drivers to read and speak English well enough to converse with the public, understand highway traffic signs and signals, respond to official inquiries, and make entries on reports and records. Enforcement of that requirement has been strengthened, and drivers who can't demonstrate proficiency can be placed out of service at roadside inspections.

States also set their own rules for which languages CDL knowledge tests are offered in. Check your state driver licensing agency's current policy.

Bottom line: Spanish-language training is a comprehension tool, not a substitute for English proficiency. Use it to help drivers truly understand safety material while they build the English skills the job requires.

Why bilingual training matters to employers

  • Deeper understanding. Drivers retain complex safety concepts better when they can learn them in their strongest language.
  • Bigger candidate pool. Bilingual training opens your recruiting to more applicants.
  • Fewer failed assessments. Comprehension issues are a common reason drivers struggle with the 80% theory assessment.
  • Better retention. Drivers who feel supported in training are more likely to stay.

Best practices for a bilingual training program

  1. Offer theory with an English/Spanish toggle so drivers can move between languages.
  2. Encourage drivers to review key terms and road signs in English.
  3. Pair theory with English-language practice for inspection and roadside scenarios.
  4. Use bilingual trainers for behind-the-wheel where possible.
  5. Confirm your state's knowledge test language options before scheduling.

Every course includes an English/Spanish toggle. Offer bilingual ELDT theory to your audience from your own page.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take ELDT theory training in Spanish?
Yes. The ELDT rule does not restrict the language of instruction, and many registered providers offer Spanish theory courses. HAZWOPER OSHA Training includes an English/Spanish toggle on every course.
Do CDL drivers need to speak English?
Yes. Under 49 CFR 391.11(b)(2), commercial drivers must read and speak English sufficiently to communicate with the public, understand traffic signs, respond to official inquiries, and complete reports.
Can the CDL knowledge test be taken in Spanish?
It depends on the state. Each state licensing agency sets its own policy on knowledge test languages, so check with yours.

This guide is for general information and reflects FMCSA ELDT requirements as of October 25, 2026. Regulations and state policies change; confirm current requirements with FMCSA and your state driver licensing agency before making training or compliance decisions.
