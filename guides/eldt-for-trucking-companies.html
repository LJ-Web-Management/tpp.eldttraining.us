ELDT for Trucking Companies: Onboarding New CDL Drivers Faster

For carriers and private fleets, ELDT is more than a compliance box. It's a lever for recruiting new drivers, upgrading the ones you already have, and getting everyone on the road sooner. Here's how fleets are using it.

Updated October 25, 2026 3 min read By the ELDTTraining.us team, an FMCSA TPR-listed ELDT theory provider

Key Takeaways

  • Company-sponsored CDL programs, B-to-A upgrades, and new hazmat or passenger endorsements all trigger ELDT.
  • Moving theory online lets recruits start training the day they accept an offer, shrinking the gap between hire and first load.
  • Fleets can offer ELDT theory to their own drivers and to their broader network, and earn revenue doing it.

Where ELDT shows up in a fleet

Even if you only hire experienced drivers, ELDT probably touches your operation more than you think:

  • Company-sponsored CDL programs that train new drivers from scratch need Class A or Class B ELDT.
  • Upgrades from Class B to Class A, for example moving local straight-truck drivers into tractor-trailers, require B-to-A ELDT.
  • New endorsements, such as adding hazmat to win a fuel or chemical contract, require Hazmat (H) ELDT theory.
  • Yard and warehouse employees moving into driving roles need full ELDT for their first CDL.

Shrinking time from offer to first load

Every day between a recruit accepting an offer and driving revenue miles is a day they might take another offer. Classroom theory is often a bottleneck: recruits wait for the next cohort, travel to a terminal, and sit through days of instruction.

Online theory changes the sequence. A recruit can start ELDT theory on their phone the evening they accept your offer, finish at their own pace, and arrive at behind-the-wheel training ready to drive. Your trainers spend their time in the truck, which is where they add the most value.

Upskilling your existing drivers

Your best future Class A and hazmat drivers may already be on your payroll. A structured upskilling path might look like this:

  1. Identify Class B drivers or yard staff with clean records who want to move up.
  2. Have them complete B-to-A or Class A theory online during downtime.
  3. Pair them with an in-house trainer or CDL school for behind-the-wheel training.
  4. Add the Hazmat theory course for drivers on hazmat lanes.

Promoting from within improves retention and lowers recruiting costs, and drivers notice when a company invests in their careers.

Supporting a bilingual workforce

Many fleets employ drivers and warehouse staff whose first language is Spanish. A course with an English/Spanish toggle helps them learn complex material, like hours of service and cargo securement, in the language they understand best, while still preparing for English-language road signs, inspections, and communication. Frequently asked questions

Do company-sponsored CDL training programs need to follow ELDT?
Yes. Any driver obtaining a first-time Class A or B CDL must complete ELDT from a provider on the FMCSA Training Provider Registry, whether the training is paid for by the driver or the company.
Does upgrading from a Class B to a Class A CDL require ELDT?
Yes. The Class B to Class A upgrade has its own ELDT theory and behind-the-wheel requirements.
Can a trucking company buy ELDT theory training for its drivers?
Yes. Through an ELDT Partner Program page, a company can let drivers purchase training themselves or purchase it on their behalf.

This guide is for general information and reflects FMCSA ELDT requirements as of October 25, 2026. Regulations and state policies change; confirm current requirements with FMCSA and your state driver licensing agency before making training or compliance decisions.
