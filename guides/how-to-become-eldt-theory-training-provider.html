How to Become an ELDT Theory Training Provider (and a Faster Alternative)

Every new CDL driver needs ELDT theory, and that steady demand makes it tempting to become a registered provider. Before you commit, here's an honest look at what FMCSA requires, what it really costs to run a compliant program, and the shortcut many organizations take instead.

Updated October 25, 2026 4 min read By the ELDTTraining.us team, an FMCSA TPR-listed ELDT theory provider

Key Takeaways

  • To deliver ELDT theory you must meet the Part 380 curriculum, instructor, and assessment requirements and register each location on the FMCSA Training Provider Registry.
  • The ongoing work, including course updates, assessments, TPR submissions within two business days, three years of records, and audit readiness, is where most of the real cost lives.
  • Partnering with a provider that's already registered lets you offer ELDT theory through a dedicated landing page and share the revenue, without carrying the compliance burden.

Why organizations consider becoming a provider

ELDT theory is a required purchase for every first-time Class A or Class B driver, every Class B to Class A upgrade, and every first-time Passenger, School Bus, or Hazmat endorsement. If you already have a pipeline of those drivers (as a CDL school, staffing agency, carrier, association, or workforce program), capturing the theory step feels like an obvious move.

There are two ways to do it: register as a training provider yourself, or partner with one that is already registered. Let's look at the first path in detail.

What FMCSA requires of a theory provider

Training providers self-certify on the Training Provider Registry that they meet the standards in 49 CFR Part 380, Subpart G. For theory instruction, that means:

1. A complete, compliant curriculum

Your course must cover every topic in the applicable appendix: Appendix A for Class A, B for Class B, C for Passenger, D for School Bus, and E for Hazmat. That includes vehicle systems, safe operating procedures, hours of service, cargo handling, whistleblower protections, drug and alcohol rules, and more. When regulations change, your content has to change with them.

2. A proficiency assessment

Drivers must score at least 80% on a written or electronic theory assessment. You need a question bank that covers the full curriculum and a delivery method that protects test integrity.

3. Qualified instructors

Theory instructors generally must hold a CDL of the same or higher class with the applicable endorsements and have at least two years of experience driving a CMV requiring that CDL, or two years of experience as a behind-the-wheel instructor, subject to the limited exceptions in the rule. Even fully online courses need qualified instructors behind the content and available to learners.

4. TPR registration and reporting

Each training location registers on the TPR, attests that it meets every requirement, and keeps that registration current, including the required biennial update. After a driver completes training, the provider must submit the completion to the TPR by midnight of the second business day.

5. Recordkeeping and audits

Providers must retain training records for at least three years and make them available to FMCSA on request. FMCSA can audit providers and remove them from the registry for non-compliance, and it has stepped up removals of providers that can't back up their certifications.

The real cost of running a compliant program

Registration itself doesn't carry a federal fee. The cost is everything around it:

  • Curriculum development for each course you offer, often five separate courses to cover A, B, P, S, and H.
  • A learning platform that handles enrollment, progress tracking, assessments, certificates, and mobile access.
  • Translation if you serve Spanish-speaking drivers, which most fleets and schools do.
  • Payments, refunds, and customer support for drivers who get stuck at 9 p.m. on a Sunday.
  • Staff time for TPR submissions, records retention, and audit preparation.
  • Ongoing updates when FMCSA guidance or regulations change.

For an organization that trains hundreds of drivers a year and wants full control, that investment can make sense. For most organizations, the math is much harder to justify.

Which path is right for you?

Becoming a provider can make sense if training is your core business, you have instructional design and compliance staff, and you want to own every detail. Partnering makes sense if your strength is your audience (drivers, members, students, or clients) and you'd rather earn on theory training than operate it.

Many CDL schools use both: they register as a provider for behind-the-wheel training, and partner for theory. See how CDL schools outsource ELDT theory.

Frequently asked questions

Does it cost money to register on the FMCSA Training Provider Registry?
There is no federal registration fee. The costs of becoming a provider come from building and maintaining a compliant curriculum, assessments, platform, instructors, reporting, and records.
How long do ELDT training providers have to report completions?
Providers must submit a driver's completion to the Training Provider Registry by midnight of the second business day after the driver completes training.
How long must ELDT training records be kept?
Training providers must retain ELDT records for at least three years.
Can I sell ELDT theory training without becoming a registered provider?
Yes, by partnering with a registered provider. In the ELDTTraining.us ELDT Partner Program, ELDTTraining.us remains the provider and seller of record while partners get a dedicated landing page and a 50/50 revenue split.
