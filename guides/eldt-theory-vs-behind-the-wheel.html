ELDT Basics

ELDT Theory vs. Behind-the-Wheel Training: What's the Difference?

Every ELDT program has two halves: theory and behind-the-wheel. They have different requirements, different costs, and often different providers. Understanding the difference is the first step to designing a faster, cheaper training path for new drivers.

Updated October 25, 2026 3 min read By the ELDTTraining.us team, an FMCSA TPR-listed ELDT theory provider

Key Takeaways

  • Theory is knowledge instruction that can be delivered online. Behind-the-wheel is hands-on range and public road training in a real vehicle.
  • Theory requires an 80% assessment score; behind-the-wheel proficiency is judged by the instructor. Neither has a federal minimum hour requirement.
  • A driver can complete each portion with a different registered provider, and Hazmat (H) is theory only.

Theory training explained

Theory instruction covers everything a new driver needs to know. For a Class A CDL, that includes basic operation, safe operating procedures, advanced operating practices like hazard perception and skid control, vehicle systems and malfunction reporting, and non-driving activities such as hours of service, trip planning, cargo handling, and drug and alcohol rules.

Because it's knowledge-based, theory adapts well to online delivery. Drivers can work through modules at their own pace, review material before the assessment, and finish on a phone or tablet. To complete theory, a driver must score at least 80% on the assessment.

Behind-the-wheel training explained

Behind-the-wheel training covers what a new driver needs to do. It has two parts:

  • Range training on a closed course: vehicle inspections, straight-line backing, offset and alley-dock backing, parallel parking, and coupling and uncoupling (for Class A).
  • Public road training in real traffic: turns, lane changes, intersections, railroad crossings, curves, and highway driving.

There's no minimum hour requirement. The instructor decides when the driver has demonstrated proficiency in every required element. BTW requires qualified instructors, a vehicle representative of the class being trained, and a suitable range, which is why it's typically the most expensive part of CDL training.

Why many programs split theory and BTW

FMCSA allows a driver to complete theory with one registered provider and behind-the-wheel with another. Each provider reports its own portion to the Training Provider Registry. That flexibility has led to a common model:

  • Online theory from a provider that specializes in curriculum, platform, and compliance reporting.
  • Behind-the-wheel from a CDL school, carrier, district, or agency that already has trucks, buses, ranges, and instructors.

The benefits are practical: drivers can start theory immediately instead of waiting for a classroom cohort, instructors spend their time in the vehicle instead of at a whiteboard, and theory costs become predictable per driver.

The Hazmat exception

The Hazardous Materials (H) endorsement is the only ELDT category with no behind-the-wheel requirement. Drivers complete hazmat theory, and the state checks the TPR before administering the H knowledge test. That makes hazmat one of the simplest courses to deliver entirely online. Frequently asked questions

Is ELDT theory training required before behind-the-wheel?
Federal rules do not require a specific order, but both must be completed and reported to the Training Provider Registry before the CDL skills test. Many programs complete theory first so drivers arrive at behind-the-wheel training with the knowledge base in place.
Does the Hazmat endorsement require behind-the-wheel training?
No. The Hazmat (H) endorsement requires ELDT theory only.
How many hours is ELDT behind-the-wheel training?
There is no federal minimum. The instructor determines when the driver is proficient in each required range and public road skill.

This guide is for general information and reflects FMCSA ELDT requirements as of October 25, 2026. Regulations and state policies change; confirm current requirements with FMCSA and your state driver licensing agency before making training or compliance decisions.
