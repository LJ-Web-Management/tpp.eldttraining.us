ELDT Requirements for School Bus Drivers: A Guide for School Districts

Driver shortages have made every new school bus hire precious, and ELDT added a federal training step between hiring and testing. Here's what your district needs to know to keep new drivers moving from application to route as quickly as possible.

Updated October 25, 2026 3 min read By the ELDTTraining.us team at HAZWOPER OSHA Training, an FMCSA TPR-listed ELDT theory provider

Key Takeaways

  • Most new school bus drivers need ELDT for a Class B CDL plus the Passenger (P) and School Bus (S) endorsements, unless they already hold some of them.
  • Each of those has a theory portion and a behind-the-wheel portion, and the theory portion can be completed online.
  • Districts can run behind-the-wheel training in-house and use a registered online provider for theory, reducing cost and time per hire.

What new school bus drivers need

A driver operating a school bus designed to carry 16 or more people (including the driver) needs a commercial driver's license, typically a Class B, along with the Passenger (P) and School Bus (S) endorsements. Under the FMCSA Entry-Level Driver Training rule, a driver getting any of those for the first time must complete ELDT for each one before taking the corresponding skills test.

That means a brand-new hire with only a regular driver's license typically needs:

  • Class B CDL theory and behind-the-wheel training
  • Passenger (P) endorsement theory and behind-the-wheel training
  • School Bus (S) endorsement theory and behind-the-wheel training

A driver who already holds a Class B CDL with a P endorsement, for example a former transit driver, would only need the School Bus (S) training.

Check your state's rules too. States add their own school bus requirements on top of federal ELDT, such as pre-service hours, annual in-service training, background checks, and physical exams. ELDT is the federal floor, not the whole picture.

What the School Bus (S) theory curriculum covers

The School Bus endorsement curriculum (Appendix D of 49 CFR Part 380) focuses on the risks unique to transporting children, including:

  • Danger zones around the bus and use of mirrors
  • Loading and unloading procedures
  • Emergency exits and evacuation procedures
  • Railroad-highway grade crossings
  • Managing student behavior
  • Antilock braking systems and special safety considerations
  • Pre- and post-trip inspections

Like all ELDT theory, there's no minimum hour requirement. Drivers must complete the full curriculum and score at least 80% on the assessment.

The in-house model most districts use

Many districts already employ experienced trainers and own the buses needed for behind-the-wheel instruction. The challenge is usually theory: building a compliant classroom curriculum, keeping it current, scheduling sessions around new-hire start dates, and reporting completions to the Training Provider Registry.

A common solution is to split the work:

  1. Theory online through a provider listed on the TPR. New hires can start the day they're hired, on any device, at their own pace.
  2. Behind-the-wheel in-house with the district's own trainers and buses, registered on the TPR for BTW.
  3. Skills test once both portions are reported to the TPR.

Because theory and behind-the-wheel can be completed with different registered providers, this split is fully compliant and often the fastest route from hire to route.

Reducing cost and time per driver

When districts look at the true cost of a new driver, theory classroom time often stands out. Paying a trainer to deliver classroom instruction, plus paying the trainee to sit through it, adds up quickly. Online theory shifts that time to a self-paced format and frees trainers for behind-the-wheel work.

Other cost levers to consider:

  • Volume pricing. Buying seats in bulk across the school year usually beats buying one at a time.
  • SCORM packages. If your district already runs a learning management system, a SCORM version of the course keeps completions and records in your own system.
  • Bilingual courses. An English/Spanish toggle helps you recruit from a wider pool of applicants.

Questions to ask an online theory provider

  • Is the provider listed on the FMCSA Training Provider Registry, and will it report completions within two business days?
  • Does it cover every course your new hires need: Class B, Passenger, and School Bus?
  • Can drivers switch between English and Spanish?
  • Does it work on phones and tablets?
  • Can it provide a SCORM package or bulk seats for your district?
  • Who handles support when a driver gets stuck?

Frequently asked questions

Do school bus drivers need ELDT?
Yes, in most cases. Drivers getting a Class B CDL, Passenger (P) endorsement, or School Bus (S) endorsement for the first time must complete ELDT for each before taking the skills test.
Can school bus drivers complete ELDT theory online?
Yes. Theory instruction for the Class B CDL, Passenger, and School Bus endorsements can be completed online with a provider listed on the FMCSA Training Provider Registry.
Can a school district provide its own behind-the-wheel training?
Yes. Districts with qualified trainers and vehicles can register on the Training Provider Registry to deliver behind-the-wheel training and use a separate registered provider for theory.
Does ELDT replace state school bus training requirements?
No. ELDT is a federal minimum. States often add their own pre-service and in-service school bus driver training requirements.

This guide is for general information and reflects FMCSA ELDT requirements as of October 25, 2026. Regulations and state policies change; confirm current requirements with FMCSA and your state driver licensing agency before making training or compliance decisions.
